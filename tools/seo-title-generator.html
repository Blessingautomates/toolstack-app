    What this tool does

    A title tag is the single strongest on-page ranking signal you control, and it is also the headline someone decides whether to click. Google truncates it by rendered pixel width rather than character count, which is why the usual "keep it under 60 characters" advice fails on titles full of wide letters. This tool measures the actual width of your string in the font Google uses for desktop results, then reports it against the roughly 600-pixel budget.

    It also checks the things that quietly cost clicks: whether your keyword appears near the front, whether the title is long enough to say anything, whether it repeats the same word, and whether a brand name is eating the space where the descriptive phrase should be. The score is a heuristic, not a ranking prediction — Google's algorithm weighs hundreds of factors this tool cannot see, and none of them are a substitute for writing a headline a human wants to click.

    How to use it

    1. Paste the title you are considering into the field. The meters update as you type, so you can feel the pixel budget rather than count characters.
    2. Add your primary keyword. The tool checks whether it appears in the title and how close to the front it sits — a keyword at position ten is worth far less than one at position two.
    3. Add your brand name so the tool can flag it if it is consuming the space the descriptive phrase needs.
    4. Click Analyze Title for the full report: issues found, and concrete rewrites rather than vague advice.
    5. Click Copy Title when you are happy, then paste it into your CMS or template.

    Frequently asked questions

    How long should a title tag be?

    Google truncates by pixel width, not character count — roughly 600 pixels on desktop. For average-width Latin text that lands near 60 characters, but a title full of capital W and M letters can truncate at 50 while one made of narrow letters survives to 70. That is why this tool measures the actual rendered width instead of counting characters and calling it a day.

    Does the title tag affect ranking?

    Yes — more than any other on-page element. Google treats the title as a strong relevance signal and bolds the query terms it finds there, so a title containing the phrase someone searched for both ranks better and looks more clickable. It is not a place to keyword-stuff, though: repeated or unrelated terms read as spam to users and depress click-through, which is the metric that actually compounds.

    Should the brand name go at the start or the end of the title?

    Put it at the end unless the brand is the reason someone is searching. Front-loading the descriptive phrase means the part that survives truncation is the part that matches the query. Brand-first only makes sense on navigational pages where people search the company name itself — putting it first on a content page spends your most valuable pixels on the words fewest searchers typed.
